# The Golden Spike: facts, sources, and open questions

Draft for Chance's review, Nov 2026. "Fact" means at least one solid source supports it. "Story choice" means I made it up or simplified it on purpose.

## Core facts used in the story

### May 10, 1869, Promontory Summit, Utah Territory
- The last spike went in at Promontory Summit, at the north end of the Promontory Mountains. It was not at Promontory Point, the south tip of the peninsula. (NPS Golden Spike NHS)
- The plan was May 8 (that date is engraved on the gold spike). It moved to May 10 for two reasons. About 400 unpaid tie cutters held Thomas Durant's car at Piedmont, Wyoming. Rain also damaged the Devil's Gate bridge in Weber Canyon. (NPS "A Moment in Time"; NPS CLR ch. 2)
- Congress set Promontory Summit as the meeting point on April 10, 1869.
- Engines: Central Pacific No. 60 "Jupiter" burned wood and had a balloon stack. Engineer George Booth. It arrived a few minutes after 11 a.m. with Leland Stanford's train. Union Pacific No. 119 burned coal and had a straight stack. It was built by Rogers in 1868. Engineer Sam Bradford. 119 took over Durant's train after the bridge damage. (NPS "Everlasting Steam")
- The last rails: an Irish Union Pacific crew under foreman Guilford, and a Chinese Central Pacific crew in clean blue frocks under foreman H. H. Minkler. (NPS CLR; CPRR.org "Chinese at Promontory")
- Laurel tie made by West Evans, with a silver plaque. It was finished by Strahle & Hughes and had four pre-drilled holes. It was destroyed in the 1906 San Francisco fire. (NPS "Four Special Spikes"; NPS CLR)
- Four ceremonial spikes. The golden "Last Spike" came from David Hewes and was cast by the Wm. T. Garratt Foundry: 17.6 karat, about 14 oz, 5 5/8 in. It was given to Stanford University in 1892 and is now at the Cantor Arts Center. Gold is soft, so the special spikes went into pre-drilled holes and got light taps only. (NPS "Four Special Spikes")
- A regular iron spike and a regular hammer were wired to the telegraph, so each blow could click down the line. Stanford swung and missed or glanced off. Sources disagree on details (see "Open questions" below). The finished signal "D-O-N-E" went out at 12:47 p.m. local time. (NPS; NYT May 11, 1869)
- Telegraph operator: W. N. (Watson) Shilling. (NPS CLR; NYT)
- Messages, from the NYT of May 11, 1869 (Washington times): 2:27 p.m. "Almost ready. Hats off; prayer is being offered." 2:40 p.m. "We have got done praying. The spike is about to be presented." Then: "All ready now; the spike will soon be driven. The signal will be three dots for the commencement of the blows." DONE arrived at 2:47 p.m. In Washington, operator Tinker at the Western Union office (14th St & Pennsylvania Ave) had a magnetic ball (bell) wired to sound the blows. Sherman and others listened at the War Department office. New York fired 100 guns and rang Trinity Church's chimes.
- Prayer: Rev. Dr. John Todd. Grenville Dodge spoke for the UP because Durant was ill. Engines touched noses, the engineers broke champagne bottles, and No. 119 crossed the joint first, then Jupiter. A. J. Russell's photo "East and West Shaking Hands at Laying Last Rail" shows Montague and Dodge.
- Bands: the 21st Infantry band, and the Tenth Ward Band from Ogden (Latter-day Saint). (NPS CLR)
- Crowd estimates run from 500 to 3,000. Photos suggest 500–600. The story just says "hundreds."
- Strobridge invited the Chinese crew to dine in his car, and the guests cheered them. (SF Newsletter / Sacramento Bee, May 12, 1869, via CPRR.org)
- Distances: UP built 1,086 mi, CP 690 mi.

### Ten-Mile Day, April 28, 1869 (Central Pacific)
- 10 miles 56 feet in one day. Work started about 7:15 a.m. and ended about 7 p.m. There were 8 Irish rail handlers (each lifted about 125 tons in total) and hundreds of Chinese workers. Rails averaged about 560 lb. Lunch was at a camp called Victory. Jim Campbell ran an engine over the new track at about 40 mph. The telegraph crew kept pace. Charles Crocker bet Durant $10,000. (NPS "Ten Miles in One Day"; CPRR.org)
- Tea: Chinese crews drank tea made with boiled water carried in buckets on shoulder poles. They got sick less often than crews drinking raw water. (Widely reported, e.g. NPS Chinese Labor page. Softened in the story to "boiling kills germs.")

### Latter-day Saint ties
- In May 1868 Brigham Young signed a grading contract with the Union Pacific for Echo and Weber canyons. LDS contractors (e.g., Sharp & Young; Benson, Farr & West) later graded for both companies near Promontory. The two companies built parallel grades for miles because both were paid per mile. (Church History in the Fulness of Times ch. 31; NPS "Big Fill")
- The Big Fill was built by Benson, Farr & West (LDS) from about February 1869: 500+ workers, 250 teams, about 10,000 cu yd. Next to it, the UP's Big Trestle was built by Sharp & Young (LDS), begun March 28, 1869, about 85 ft high and 400 ft long. (NPS "Big Fill")
- Brigham Young did not attend. He was travelling back from southern Utah and returned May 11. Bishop John Sharp and Ogden mayor Lorin Farr represented the Church. (NPS CLR; Church News 1994; ChurchofJesusChrist.org 150th anniversary facts)
- UP paid part of what it owed Young in rails and equipment. Those helped build the Utah Central (Ogden to SLC). Groundbreaking was May 17, 1869, and it was completed January 10, 1870. Brigham Young drove its last spike, engraved "Holiness to the Lord" with a beehive. (Wikipedia "Utah Central Railroad"; utahrails.net)
- Salt Lake City on May 10, 1869: flags, bands, and artillery salutes from Arsenal Hill and City Hall. Stores closed, and 6,000–7,000 people met in the New Tabernacle at 2 p.m. (Deseret News, May 12, 1869; Reeder ms.)
- Deseret Telegraph trained women as operators. (Utah History to Go) The named SLC operator in the story is fictional (see below).

### Handcart companies
- Ten handcart companies crossed from 1856 to 1860. The Ellsworth company (the first) left Iowa City on June 9, 1856 and arrived in SLC on Sept 26 with about 274 people. Many came from England on the ship Enoch Train. Annie (Ann) Powell, age 4, walked beside Captain Edmund Ellsworth, who promised her bread and butter. (Ensign, July 2000, "They Walked 1,300 Miles")
- They covered 10–28 miles a day. They burned buffalo chips for fuel. The carts were made of green lumber with no axle grease, and they squeaked and wore out. Repairs used tin and leather (e.g., tin hub boxes). (Palimpsest; Ensign)
- The handcart song by J. D. T. McAllister: "Some must push and some must pull, / As we go marching up the hill, / As merrily on the way we go / Until we reach the valley, oh!"

### Telegraph and time
- Morse code in 1869 meant American Morse, not today's International code. D = dash dot dot, O = dot (space) dot, N = dash dot, E = dot. The story uses the American version.
- Every town kept its own sun time in 1869. Railroad standard time zones started November 18, 1883.
- The first machine that could record sound and play it back was Edison's phonograph in 1877.

## Story choices (made up or simplified on purpose)
- Evelyn, Dr. Finch, the TimeBand, and its rules (3 charges, 1-hour trip clock, safety return) are fiction carried over from the Moon story.
- These people are fictional or unnamed composites: the SLC woman telegraph operator, the old woman in SLC who walked with a handcart, the handcart repair man, the friendly firemen, the tea carrier, and the young bandsman.
- Dialogue lines for historical people are invented but in character. Exception: the telegraph messages are quoted from the NYT.
- "Just after noon" is used for DONE. Exact local times differ between sources: 12:47 p.m. at Promontory; SLC reported about 12:32 by its own clocks.
- The Tenth Ward Band playing at the exact moment of DONE is plausible but not confirmed.

## Open questions / uncertainties
1. LDS presence: NPS CLR names John Sharp, Lorin Farr, and the Tenth Ward Band as present. A 1994 Church News piece suggested few or no Latter-day Saints were present. The story follows NPS.
2. Who drove the last iron spike after Stanford and Durant missed: sources variously say Strobridge, Reed, a UP/CP worker, or a Chinese worker. The story says "a worker."
3. Stanford "missed the spike and hit the tie" (common telling) vs. "struck the tie/rail" (NPS). The story says he missed and hit the tie.
4. The exact tin repair method on the Ellsworth carts is not certain. The story keeps it simple: "a strip of tin."
5. Big Fill volume: NPS says about 10,000 cu yd. Some secondary sources say more.
6. The Washington clock: I gave the 2:47 arrival as Washington local time, per NYT.
7. The handcart photo page uses an 1903 engraving because no public-domain photograph of an actual handcart company was found.
